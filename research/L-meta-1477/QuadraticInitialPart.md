# Quadratic parses in the order-four incidence circuit

Let G be the incidence graph in L-meta-1477. It has two parts of size 21 and 105 edges. The five translates from any row to its line indices have offsets 0, 5, 7, 17 and 20 modulo 21. Their twenty ordered nonzero differences are all distinct modulo 21. This proves that two distinct rows meet in exactly one column and that G contains no four-cycle. Each offset separately defines a perfect matching. Deleting at most four edges leaves at least one of the five matchings intact.

Take any monotone fan-in-two circuit for the edge function. Fold its constant gates, missing reads and unary aliases, and remove unused gates before defining its parses below. In each parse of a gate choose one child of an OR and both children of an AND, counting leaf occurrences with multiplicity. Let k be the number of graph edges for which this circuit has no output parse consisting of the two distinct endpoints, each occurring exactly once.

**Proposition.** A circuit with k at most three has at least 125 gates. If k is one or two it has at least 126 gates. In particular, a counterexample of size 124 requires at least four different edges on which every successful parse repeats a variable.

## The homogeneous part

Assume k is less than 105. Give an input degree one. Give OR the minimum child degree and AND the sum of the two child degrees. This is the minimum number of leaves in a parse. At any OR of unequal degrees replace the gate by its lower-degree operand. Remove unused gates. At each gate this operation keeps precisely its parses of minimum degree. Every two-leaf output parse uses an edge of G because evaluating its leaves as true must make the output true. A repeated leaf would accept a singleton, which is impossible. The output has degree two and the resulting circuit computes exactly the edge function of the subgraph H obtained by deleting the k edges. Denote by h the reduction in gate count, including the cost of each OR replaced by a wire.

Every AND in the remaining output cone computes a product of two ORs of variables. Its two sets of variables form a complete bipartite subgraph of H, so they constitute a star. The degree-two gates after these products are purely ORs. Choose a center for each product, breaking ties arbitrarily for a single-edge product. Write t for the number of products and assign each edge of H to one product covering it. For each center v let d(v) be its number of assigned edges and t(v) its number of products. In the computation of the neighbor sets for these products at least d(v) distinct leaves reach at most t(v) outputs, so at least d(v)−t(v) binary OR gates are required. A gate with at least two distinct leaves in its support cannot occur in the neighbor sets for two different centers, since G is bipartite and four-cycle-free. Any OR computing just one variable can be replaced by that input before applying this count. This simplification in the homogeneous circuit gives a lower bound on its original size as well. Summing over centers gives at least |E(H)|−t lower OR gates. Collecting t product outputs requires at least t−1 upper OR gates. The centers form a vertex cover. Thus the size of the homogeneous circuit is at least |E(H)|+τ(H)−1. If k≤4, the matching above shows that τ(H)=21, so the original size is at least 125−k+h.

## The cost of losing a two-leaf parse

If k>0, at least one unequal-degree OR is removed. Otherwise the original circuit is homogeneous and its edge witnesses all have two leaves. There is also a discarded AND gate. On any missing edge uv take an original successful parse with leaves in {u,v}. It must contain both variables. Choose an AND with no AND below it in this parse. Each of its two selected child parses is a single leaf, so the gate has degree two and one of its minimum monomials is uu, uv or vv. None is present at the homogeneous output. A surviving degree-two gate can reach that output only through ORs retaining its minimum monomials. The chosen AND therefore does not survive. Thus h≥2.

In fact **h=2 implies k≤1**. The two removed gates must be the unequal-degree OR r and the discarded AND g just described. All other gates are in the homogeneous output cone, after bypassing r. The larger child of r must be g. To see this, a larger child elsewhere in the surviving homogeneous cone has degree at most two. If it were an existing degree-two wire, the other discarded gate g could not reach the original output without entering r or a surviving gate upstream of r. Since there is no other unequal-degree OR, such a path would send g's minimum monomials through homogeneous gates to that retained degree-two wire, contradicting its survival. More directly, follow any original path from g toward the output until its first loss in the homogeneous trimming. This must be the larger input of r. The segment up to that input contains no retained gate, since a retained gate other than r has retained inputs after trimming. There is no third discarded gate, so the segment is a single edge. Thus g is the larger child of r and precedes r. The smaller child is a linear form L and the two inputs to g are unmodified linear forms P and Q. Both inputs are either primary inputs or retained gates.

In the homogeneous computation mark the linear occurrences originating at the replaced r. A marked degree-one OR has the extra summand PQ in the original computation. No quadratic product can have marked linear inputs on both sides: they would both contain the nonempty L, accepting a singleton in H. At each product with one marked input, call its opposite input a companion. Its homogeneous computation accepts every pair between L and the companion. Let R be the union of all companions, interpreted as an OR of variables. This set is nonempty because r matters to a missing edge. Propagating the extra summand through these products and the final ORs shows that the original function is exactly H OR PQR. All pairs between L and R belong to H.

Each of P, Q and R is a set in a single bipartite part. This is immediate for a singleton. Every retained linear gate reaches a homogeneous product, whose opposite operand supplies a vertex adjacent in H to all its variables. This proves the assertion for P and Q and supplies a common H-neighbor whenever the set has at least two members. A singleton primary input used only by the discarded AND may be isolated in H. The edges between L and R give the same assertions for R. The three sets cannot all be on the same side, since the assignment setting that side true and the other side false would make PQR true. Let U and V be the two on the same side and B the one on the opposite side. The two-element assignments added by PQR are contained in the rectangle (U∩V) × B, and every pair in this rectangle is an edge of G.

If |B|≥2, B has a common neighbor in H. Since G is four-cycle-free, this is the unique common neighbor of any two distinct elements of B, and every vertex of U∩V coincides with it. Every edge of the rectangle already belongs to H. If |U∩V|≥2, the common H-neighbor of U is similarly the only possible member of B, and all rectangle edges are already present. A nonempty new-edge set is therefore confined to a one-by-one rectangle. This proves h=2 implies k≤1.

For k=0 the homogeneous bound is 125. For k=1 one has h≥2; for k=2,3 one has h≥3. Substitution in 125−k+h proves all claims. The proposition leaves open circuits with four or more missing two-leaf parses.

The same proof applies to the order-three plane: its four disjoint perfect matchings preserve the covering number for all three permitted deletions. More generally any balanced bipartite regular graph of degree at least four with no four-cycle has equally many vertices on each side and decomposes into perfect matchings. Provided at most three edges lack a two-leaf parse, the proof gives the star-cover lower bound |E(G)|+τ(G)−1.
